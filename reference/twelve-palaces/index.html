The twelve palaces

A Zi Wei Dou Shu chart divides a life into twelve areas and places them from your birth hour. The palace supplies the subject and the stars sitting in it supply the tendency, which is why the same star reads differently in each of the twelve.

The twelve

Computed · In reading order

The order runs from the life palace, which classical practice reads first, round to the parents palace. Where the palaces land on the square changes with every birth; the order they are read in does not.

The six facing pairs

Computed · 對宮

No palace is read alone. Each one sits directly across the square from another, and the pair is read together. It is also the rule that rescues an empty palace: with no major star of its own, a palace is read through the stars of the one facing it.

Where the body palace can sit

Computed · 6 of 12

身宮, the body palace, is a second emphasis laid over one of the twelve. It marks where a person's effort concentrates in adult life, and it can only land on six of them. The life palace says who someone is; the body palace says where they pour themselves.

Twelve areas, not twelve verdicts

A palace is a subject. It says what an area of a life is, and it says nothing on its own about how that area goes. The stars sitting in it supply that, and how brightly they sit supplies the strength. This is why the same star means different things in different palaces, and why a palace cannot be read from its name.

It is also why counting is the wrong instinct. Classical practice weighs how well a star is placed rather than how many are present, so one major star at its brightest carries more than three dim ones.

An empty palace is normal

About half the palaces in a typical chart hold no major star. The tradition calls that 空宮 and treats it as ordinary rather than as an absence of life in that area. The rule is to read the palace through the stars of the one opposite it.

That borrowed reading is genuinely weaker than a star sitting in place, and a reading that presents it at full confidence is overstating what the method gives. Where a palace that matters to your question is empty, the honest answer names the limit.
